TTC Community

From fragmented conversations to lasting belonging — a concept for a pseudonymous support community where people navigating trying to conceive can be known without being publicly exposed.

Project TypeSelf-initiated Concept
RoleProduct Manager
FocusCommunity · Safety · Retention
StatusHypothesis-led · Not yet validated
Core product hypothesis
Anonymous enough to feel safe. Contextual enough to feel known.
01 · The opportunity

The internet already has TTC conversations. What it often lacks is continuity.

People navigating TTC can find moments of support in social-media comments, forums and group chats. The product opportunity I wanted to explore was not simply “another place to talk,” but whether persistent pseudonymous identity, journey context and recurring relationships could turn fragmented interactions into a genuine sense of belonging.

Concept question → Can a community make support feel continuous without making vulnerability feel public?

This is a product hypothesis, not a research finding. The concept has not yet been validated through external user interviews, so the case study focuses on the system I would design and the assumptions I would test.

02 · Product principle

Privacy shouldn't require becoming a stranger.

Full public identity can make sensitive conversations harder. Total anonymity can make relationships feel disposable. TTC Community explores a middle ground: persistent pseudonymous profiles that protect real-world identity while allowing trust and familiarity to accumulate over time.

Pseudonymous by default

Members choose a persistent display identity and avatar rather than exposing their real name, workplace, family network or public social profile.

Context without exposure

Members can voluntarily share lightweight journey context — for example IVF, between cycles or trying for a particular length of time — so they do not have to retell their story in every conversation.

The goal isn't anonymity for anonymity's sake. It is to let someone feel recognised inside the community without making their fertility journey publicly identifiable.
03 · Designing emotional agency

People should control what support they want — and what they are ready to receive.

TTC contains emotionally conflicting moments. One member may desperately need a success story; another may have received devastating news that morning. Instead of silencing either person, the product can give both more control over the experience.

POST INTENT

Say what you need

Posts can carry lightweight context such as Vent, Advice wanted, No advice please, Treatment update, Loss or Success.

Expression · Context
CONTENT CONTROL

Choose what you can receive

Members can temporarily mute emotionally difficult categories without asking other people to hide their experiences.

Agency · Emotional safety
PRIVATE CONTACT

DMs require consent

Private messaging begins with a request. The recipient decides whether to accept or decline before a private conversation can start.

Boundaries · Consent
Design principle
You control how much of yourself you reveal, what kind of support you want, and what you're emotionally ready to receive.
04 · Community architecture

Start concentrated. Fragment only when the community earns it.

A community can have excellent features and still fail if every room is empty. I would begin with one active general community rather than launching dozens of journey-specific groups on day one.

01One shared home

A general TTC community concentrates activity and gives every new member somewhere alive to enter.

02Limited focused spaces

Only 2–3 optional journey spaces would be introduced where distinct needs and sufficient activity justify them.

03Persistent presence

Familiar pseudonyms, journey context, recurring threads and visible activity help relationships build instead of resetting with every post.

04Participation at different energies

Members can lurk, vent, ask, encourage or simply return to familiar people. Contribution does not have to look the same every day.

05 · The cold-start problem

A beautiful empty community is still an empty community.

The first product risk is liquidity: why would the first members stay if there is nobody to talk to? Rather than acquiring isolated users one by one, I would seed the community through existing TTC social groups and trusted admins.

Cohort seeding

Partner with a small number of existing TTC community admins and invite clusters of members together. Familiarity travels with the cohort, giving the new space immediate human activity and social proof.

AI as facilitator — never a fake member

Clearly labelled platform prompts can spark check-ins, polls, welcome threads and conversations during quiet periods. AI should create the spark, not impersonate people or manufacture trust.

A useful health signal: over time, meaningful conversations should increasingly be member-initiated rather than dependent on platform prompts.
06 · Switching value

Why leave a Facebook group, WhatsApp chat or Reddit thread?

Pseudonymity alone is not enough; other platforms can approximate it. The stronger switching value comes from combining privacy with journey-aware context, emotional controls and product rules built specifically for TTC.

Known without being exposed

Persistent pseudonymous identity lets relationships accumulate without tying the journey to a public social graph.

Context without retelling

Voluntary journey context helps members understand where someone is coming from before responding.

Emotional control

Members can choose whether they have capacity for success stories, loss, advice or other sensitive categories at a particular moment.

Purpose-built boundaries

Consent-based DMs and TTC-specific moderation are part of the product system rather than rules bolted onto a general social network.

07 · Moderation & medical safety

Protect expression. Intervene at harm.

The community should not cap difficult emotions. Members need room to talk honestly about grief, anger, jealousy, treatment failure and exhaustion. The safety boundary appears when lived experience turns into medical instruction for someone else.

Allowed: sharing experience

“I took this medication during my treatment and this is what happened to me.” Members can discuss their own care and experiences without having to sanitize the reality of TTC.

Intervene: prescribing to others

“You should take this medication/dose” crosses into medical direction. Potentially prescriptive comments can be flagged, warned and escalated for repeated or dangerous violations.

The moderation target is harmful instruction — not uncomfortable emotion.

Verified medical contributors may provide general educational context, but peer status does not turn personal experience into medical authority.

08 · Designing for graduation

What happens when a member gets pregnant?

Pregnancy is a positive outcome for the member, but pregnancy content can be painful for someone still in treatment or coping with loss. The product should protect both without treating belonging as conditional.

01Identity persists

The member keeps the same pseudonymous profile, relationships and history.

02Status can evolve

Journey context can voluntarily change to something like “Pregnant after TTC” without becoming an unavoidable badge in every interaction.

03Pregnancy content is opt-in

Pregnancy-specific updates belong in a separate layer so members who cannot receive that content do not encounter it unexpectedly.

04Belonging can continue

Graduates can still support conversations where appropriate; success does not automatically erase relationships built during the journey.

For this product, graduation from the core TTC experience can represent success — not churn to be prevented at all costs.
09 · MVP hypothesis

Test belonging before building everything.

The first version should answer one question: will people repeatedly return to a pseudonymous TTC community because persistent relationships and relevant context make them feel less alone?

10 · What I would measure

Not “How many people joined?” — “Did a community actually form?”

Because this concept is unvalidated, these are proposed measures for a pilot rather than results. I would combine behavioural signals with direct measures of perceived support and safety.

D7 / D30

Retention by cohort: do members return after the novelty of joining wears off?

Reply rate

What percentage of member posts receive a meaningful human response?

Member-led %

How much conversation begins organically rather than through platform/AI prompts?

Belonging

Short recurring survey: “I feel understood and supported here.”

I would also watch time-to-first-response, repeat contributors, lurker-to-contributor movement, reports/blocks, medical-advice violations and the health of each focused space before allowing further fragmentation.
11 · What still needs validation

This is a product exploration, not a finished answer.

The concept currently rests on assumptions that should be challenged before a build: whether persistent pseudonymity creates meaningful switching value, whether journey context feels supportive rather than exposing, whether emotional filters help without creating avoidance, and whether people will move from existing social groups into a purpose-built community.

Next step → validate the behaviour, not just the feature list.

I would begin with interviews and a small seeded pilot, then test whether members actually form recurring relationships, whether the moderation model feels safe, and which community structures deserve to exist based on real participation.

12 · Reflection

The product isn't the feed. It's the conditions around the people in it.

“Community value is created by members. Product's job is to make that value safer, easier to find and more likely to compound.”

This exploration changed my thinking from “What features should a TTC community have?” to “What conditions make a vulnerable community worth returning to?” That moved the work toward liquidity, identity, consent, emotional agency, moderation and lifecycle design.

The biggest unresolved question is also the most important one: whether the proposed experience is meaningfully better than the behaviours people already have. I would not treat the concept as validated until real users show that they return for the relationships and context — not simply because the idea sounds supportive.
