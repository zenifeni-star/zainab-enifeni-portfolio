00 · My role and contribution

My work connected discovery to a decision the team could act on.

I worked as the Product Manager on CareNest within a PM-programme simulation. The sprint notes identify me as PM and record the research, prioritization, requirements and prototype work.

My documented contribution

  • Prepared the interview guide and recruited six participants through an existing network and referrals.
  • Conducted and synthesized six interviews, then reframed the problem when the immunization hypothesis was not supported.
  • Compared candidate features using RICE and documented the prioritization rationale.
  • Authored the post-research PRD, including ten user stories, Given/When/Then acceptance criteria and non-functional requirements.
  • Documented sprint scope and translated the selected flows into wireframes and prototype work.

Project boundary

This was a programme simulation and prototype project, not a launched healthcare product. The evidence demonstrates discovery, product decisions, requirements and documented prototype delivery. It does not demonstrate clinical validation, external usability success, improved maternal confidence or achieved retention.

01 · Starting hypothesis

I began with a solution: immunization tracking.

My initial hypothesis was that Nigerian mothers of infants aged 0–12 months needed a better way to manage vaccine schedules. Rather than move directly into design, I used six structured interviews to understand how mothers managed the first year of care, where uncertainty appeared, and what support systems they already relied on.

Original hypothesis → Immunization tracking

The goal was to test whether this was a meaningful unmet need—not to prove the original idea right.

02 · Discovery

The interviews challenged my original assumption.

The strongest pattern was uncertainty in everyday care. Mothers were surrounded by information, but still unsure whether they were feeding and caring for their babies correctly. Immunization, by contrast, was generally manageable within this small sample.

6/6

Described uncertainty about whether they were caring for their infant correctly.

5/6

Raised maternal-confidence concerns without prompting.

6/6

Identified feeding as a significant source of uncertainty.

6/6

Described conflicting advice from sources such as family, WhatsApp, Google and clinic staff.

1/6

Raised immunization organically; all six described their scheduling approach as manageable when asked directly.

4/6

Had downloaded and later stopped using at least one baby-related app because it felt irrelevant, overwhelming or gave little reason to return.

The problem was not simply a shortage of information. It was uncertainty about what information to trust—and what to do next.
Sample: six Nigerian mothers of infants aged 2–9 months; four were first-time mothers and two were not. Interviews were completed in a single-day sprint, with recruitment through an existing network and referrals. The interview summary lists Lagos, Osun and Port Harcourt, while the Full PRD lists Lagos, Ibadan and Kano. I have omitted locations because the records conflict. These findings are directional and should not be generalized to the wider market.
03 · The pivot

I changed the problem before I changed the screens.

The research shifted CareNest from a vaccine-reminder concept toward maternal confidence: helping mothers make everyday care decisions with guidance that feels relevant to their baby's stage and context.

01I assumed

Mothers needed help remembering and managing immunization schedules.

02I listened

Six interviews surfaced feeding uncertainty, conflicting advice and persistent doubts about everyday care.

03I reframed

The core problem became confidence in deciding how to care for a baby—not remembering a vaccine date.

04I focused

Age-based feeding guidance became the clearest practical starting point, with a daily check-in as a supporting touchpoint.

Reframed problem
Nigerian mothers of infants aged 0–12 months can lack confidence that they are caring for their baby correctly—particularly around feeding, development and finding trustworthy, contextually relevant guidance.

The interviews supported a qualitative change in direction. They did not establish market-wide prevalence or prove that the proposed product would solve the problem.

04 · Prioritisation

RICE translated the new direction into a feature sequence.

05 · Existing alternatives

Some of the strongest alternatives were already part of mothers' routines.

The interview summary identified WhatsApp groups, Google, family members and clinic staff as information sources. These options were familiar and immediate, even when advice conflicted. CareNest therefore had to offer more than another library of content: it needed to make the next useful action easier to identify.

What mothers already used

WhatsApp groups, Google searches, family advice and clinic staff were part of the existing information mix.

What CareNest needed to earn

A place in that routine by making age-relevant guidance easier to find, understand and act on—without presenting generic information as a personal answer.

06 · Scope and delivery

I separated the broader requirements from what the sprint record says was prototyped.

The project documents do not describe one consistent MVP baseline. Rather than collapse them into a single “approved MVP,” I separate requirements, prioritization recommendations and reported delivery.

Proposed requirements

The post-research Full PRD marks itself approved by programme coaches and lists feeding guidance, daily check-in and a basic community feed as MVP scope. The Week 6 PRD uses a different Core / Supporting / Delight classification and includes a broader feature set.

Prioritization recommendation

The RICE document recommends feeding guidance and check-in first, with community in Phase 2. This differs from the Full PRD's MVP list; no change log establishes which scope version superseded the other.

Reported delivery

Sprint notes report wireframes for feeding guidance and check-in, followed by a Lovable prototype covering onboarding, feeding guidance and check-in. Community reached wireframes, not a completed prototype flow.

08 · Designing for context

“Relevant” was a product requirement, not just a positioning line.

The Full PRD specified constraints intended to make the experience usable in the target context. These were requirements, not verified performance results.

Android first

The PRD selected Android as the MVP platform, with iOS listed for a later phase.

3G-conscious performance

The requirement specified a load time under three seconds on a standard Nigerian 3G connection.

Low data usage

Core guidance and check-in experiences were specified to function under 5 MB per session.

Trust and accessibility

The PRD specified Nigeria-specific content labels, reviewer credentials, minimum 16 px text, high contrast and screen-reader compatibility.

09 · Measurement and validation

I separated the original targets from a clearer proposed value measure.

The PRD's figures are month-one targets, not achieved results. I would also make the North Star more directly reflect a completed care action rather than an app open.

Proposed measurement refinement · not in the original PRD
Weekly Guided Care Actions (WGCA)

Count distinct users who complete at least one qualifying action in a rolling seven-day period: either finish an age-relevant guidance item, or submit a daily check-in and view its recommendation.

Weekly Guided Care Action Rate = WGCA ÷ weekly active registered users × 100.

Proposed validation: run five moderated prototype sessions with mothers of infants aged 0–12 months. Five sessions provide early usability evidence, not statistical proof.

10 · Limitations and evidence boundaries

What this project demonstrates—and what it does not.

CareNest demonstrates a research-led product decision and documented prototype work. The evidence has clear limits.

Small directional sample

Six interviews completed in one day, with participants recruited through an existing network and referrals. The findings are not a market prevalence estimate.

Conflicting source details

The interview summary and Full PRD list different interview locations. Locations are omitted pending verification.

Unresolved scope history

The RICE recommendations and PRD feature classifications differ. No change log establishes the definitive final MVP baseline.

Estimated prioritization inputs

RICE Reach has an ambiguous unit in the source document. Scores are retained as planning estimates, not measured demand.

No external validation results

The reviewed files do not contain moderated usability results, production analytics or measured confidence and retention outcomes.

No clinical validation claim

The PRD specified reviewer credentials, but the reviewed evidence does not establish completed clinical-content review or clinical efficacy.

12 · Reflection

What CareNest taught me about early product work.

“Don't fall in love with the solution strongly enough to ignore the problem.”

CareNest began with an idea I could easily have defended. The interviews gave me a reason to change direction before more time was invested in the wrong feature. That was the most important product decision in the project.

I also learned to keep three things separate: what research suggests, what prioritization recommends and what a prototype demonstrates. RICE can make tradeoffs visible, but it cannot replace discovery. A prototype can make a hypothesis tangible, but it cannot prove user value without testing.
