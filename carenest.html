CareNest

When research told me my idea was wrong — and turned an immunization-tracking concept into a Nigeria-first daily confidence companion for mothers of babies aged 0–12 months.

RoleProduct Manager
Research6 User Interviews
FocusDiscovery · Strategy · MVP

The product lesson
The best product decision I made was choosing not to build the product I started with.
01 · Starting hypothesis

I began with a solution: immunization tracking.

My initial hypothesis was that Nigerian mothers of infants aged 0–12 months needed a better way to manage vaccine schedules. Rather than move directly into design, I used six structured interviews to test whether that problem was painful enough to deserve a product.

Original hypothesis → Immunization tracking

The research goal was not to prove the idea right. It was to understand how mothers actually managed the first year of care, where uncertainty appeared, and what they were already doing to cope.

02 · Discovery

The interviews rejected my original assumption.

Immunization was raised organically by only 1 of 6 participants. When asked directly, all six described vaccine scheduling as manageable through health cards, spouses or family support. The stronger pattern was uncertainty: mothers were surrounded by information but still unsure whether they were caring for their babies correctly.

6/6

Mothers described conflicting advice from family, WhatsApp, Google or clinic sources.

6/6

Feeding emerged as a recurring area of uncertainty and practical concern.

1/6

Raised immunization organically — evidence that my starting hypothesis was weak.

4/6

Had abandoned at least one baby app because it felt irrelevant, overwhelming or gave no reason to return.

The problem wasn't a shortage of information. It was a shortage of confidence in what information to trust — and what to do next.
03 · The pivot

I changed the problem before I changed the screens.

The research shifted CareNest from a vaccine-reminder concept to a maternal-confidence product: structured, age-specific support designed around the realities of Nigerian motherhood.

01I assumed

Mothers needed help remembering and managing immunization schedules.

02I listened

Six interviews surfaced feeding uncertainty, conflicting advice, emotional pressure and distrust.

03I reframed

The core job became helping mothers feel capable of making everyday baby-care decisions.

04I narrowed

The MVP focused on repeatable daily value rather than trying to solve every parenting need at once.

Reframed problem
How might CareNest help Nigerian mothers move from “Am I doing this right?” to informed, everyday confidence?
04 · Prioritisation

The original idea didn't survive RICE either.

I used RICE scoring after research to compare candidate features instead of allowing attachment to the original concept to determine the roadmap.

9.6 · Age-Based Feeding Guidance

Highest score. It addressed the most consistent practical pain and could provide clear, repeatable utility.

8.4 · Daily Confidence Check-In

A lightweight daily touchpoint designed around the emotional problem and the need for a reason to return.

1.25 · Immunization Reminder

The original concept ranked lowest and was deprioritized after the evidence showed mothers already had workable systems.

Research changed the problem. Prioritisation made sure the roadmap changed with it.
05 · Competitive thinking

One of the most important competitors wasn't another app.

Competitive analysis became more useful when I stopped looking only for products that looked like CareNest and started looking at the behaviours mothers already relied on.

The visible competitors

Baby trackers, parenting apps and information products compete on features, content and convenience.

The invisible competitor

WhatsApp mother groups. They already provided immediacy, familiarity and social reassurance — even when the information itself could be inconsistent.

Compete with behaviour, not just products.

CareNest needed to earn a place in an existing support routine. A feature list alone would not be enough to change where mothers instinctively went when they needed reassurance.

06 · MVP

I scoped the first experience around daily value.

The approved post-research MVP centered on Age-Based Feeding Guidance, a Daily Confidence Check-In and a Basic Community Feed. During wireframing, I deliberately focused first on the two core flows — feeding guidance and check-in — and deferred community work to avoid scope creep.

CORE VALUE

Age-Based Feeding Guidance

Guidance changes with the baby's age so mothers see what is relevant now rather than searching through generic information.

Research-led · Highest RICE
RETENTION

Daily Confidence Check-In

A low-effort daily prompt designed to acknowledge how the mother is feeling and provide one useful, supportive next step.

Daily habit · Emotional value
COMMUNITY

Basic Community Feed

A moderated feed connecting mothers around relevant stages without turning the MVP into an unstructured group chat.

Scoped · Moderated
The functional prototype covered Feeding Guidance, Daily Check-In and Onboarding. Community wireframes were completed separately as the scope expanded in the following sprint.
07 · From discovery to prototype

The research flowed into requirements, not just a presentation.

I carried the pivot through the product-development chain: research synthesis, prioritisation, strategy, personas, PRD, acceptance criteria, wireframes, sprint planning and a functional interactive prototype.

01Research

Interview guide → six interviews → synthesis → pivot decision.

02Strategy

Personas → competitive analysis → RICE → North Star and product strategy.

03Definition

PRD → 10 user stories → Given/When/Then acceptance criteria → non-functional requirements.

04Prototype

Wireframes → scoped sprint work → interactive onboarding, feeding and check-in experience.

08 · Designing for the Nigerian context

“Relevant” was a product requirement, not a branding line.

Interviewees had already abandoned baby apps that felt disconnected from their reality. The requirements therefore treated local context, low-bandwidth use and trust signals as part of the experience itself.

Android first

The MVP prioritized the dominant device context for the target users.

3G-conscious performance

Core experiences were specified to load quickly and keep session data usage low.

Nigeria-specific guidance

Content was intended to be locally relevant and visibly reviewed rather than presented as generic parenting advice.

Accessible core flows

Minimum font sizing, contrast and screen-reader compatibility were included in the requirements.

09 · Measuring the hypothesis

The metrics were designed to test whether confidence could become a habit.

These were MVP targets, not achieved post-launch results. They were defined to test whether mothers returned, used the core experiences and found the product genuinely useful.

30%

Target share of registered users active daily — the proposed North Star signal for habit formation.

>40%

7-day retention target, testing whether CareNest survived the early abandonment window.

>60%

Target Daily Check-In completion among active users.

>4/5

Target user-satisfaction score as a qualitative signal that the confidence problem was being addressed.

The PRD also targeted >50% completion among users who opened Feeding Guidance.
10 · Reflection

What CareNest taught me about 0→1 product work.

“Don't fall in love with the solution strongly enough to ignore the problem.”

CareNest began with an idea I could easily have defended all the way into a prototype. Instead, the interviews gave me permission to discard it. That made the project stronger: the product direction came from repeated user patterns rather than my attachment to the starting concept.

It also changed how I think about competitors. Mothers were not choosing only between parenting apps; they were choosing between CareNest and habits that already felt natural — calling family, searching Google, opening WhatsApp. Product discovery became less about asking “What features do competitors have?” and more about asking “What behaviour am I asking someone to replace?”
